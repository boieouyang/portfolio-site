During the first three weeks of T127 Teaching and Learning Lab, one idea stayed with me: learning is not just the transfer of information. The difference between a novice and an expert is also about how information is organized, recognized, and used in action.

That idea helped me look back at my internship at an aviation education company, where I was exposed to flight simulation as a learning tool. At the time, I saw simulation mainly as an impressive way to make learning feel real. Now I see it as something more complex. A simulation can support learning very well, but only when it is designed around how learners think, notice, remember, and act.

> A realistic cockpit can be useful, but realism alone does not guarantee learning.

## Experts see patterns where novices see separate details

For someone experienced with flying, a cockpit dashboard may feel manageable. The instruments are not viewed as dozens of unrelated items. Altitude, airspeed, attitude, heading, warnings, and engine readings connect into meaningful patterns.

An expert does not need to treat every signal as equally new. They can quickly recognize what matters because they have built mental structures from repeated experience.

For a beginner, the same dashboard can feel completely different. Every number, switch, light, sound, and instruction may seem important. The learner may not yet know what to ignore, what to watch first, or how one piece of information relates to another.

This helped me understand the novice–expert distinction in a more concrete way. Expertise is not only about "knowing more." It is also about seeing differently. Experts organize knowledge in ways that help them act. Novices often hold information as separate pieces, which makes action slower and more mentally demanding.

That difference matters when designing learning experiences. If a beginner enters a highly realistic simulator too early, the experience may look authentic but feel confusing. The learner may spend more effort trying to survive the interface than understanding the task.

## Realism can increase cognitive load

Before this course, I often assumed that immersive learning becomes more effective as it becomes more realistic. Flight simulation challenged that assumption.

Realism can be powerful. It allows learners to practice in a safe space, make mistakes without real-world danger, and experience situations that are hard to recreate in actual flight. But realism also adds information. More sounds, more visual details, more controls, and more possible actions can increase cognitive load.

Cognitive load becomes a serious design issue in flight simulation because the learner's attention is limited. If too many elements compete for attention, the learner may miss the actual learning goal.

A realistic simulator may ask a novice to process:

- Instrument readings
- Verbal instructions
- Aircraft movement
- Weather conditions
- Warnings and alarms
- Checklists
- Control inputs
- Time pressure

Each item may be useful later. At the wrong stage, though, the total load can become too high.

The design question is not only, "How close can we make this to real flight?" A better question is, "What does the learner need to notice right now?"

## Behaviorism has a place in procedural learning

The course also helped me see how different learning theories can be useful at different points in the same learning environment.

Some parts of flight training are strongly connected to behaviorism. Learners may practice a procedure repeatedly, receive immediate feedback, and adjust their actions. In a simulator, this can be very effective.

For example, a learner may practice responding to a warning light. The simulator gives feedback right away. If the learner chooses the wrong action, the result appears quickly. If the learner follows the correct sequence, the system stabilizes. Over time, the learner builds a reliable response.

This seems especially important for emergency procedures. In those moments, a dependable response may matter more than an open discussion. The learner needs to act quickly and accurately.

That does not mean behaviorism explains everything about learning. It does show that repetition, feedback, and correction can be valuable when the goal is procedural accuracy.

## Cognitivism explains the need for mental models

Behaviorism alone would not be enough. A learner also needs to understand why something happens.

This is where cognitivism became more meaningful to me. Learners need mental models that help them interpret situations, not just respond to signals. If they only memorize steps, they may struggle when the situation changes slightly.

In flight simulation, a learner might know which button to press when a warning appears. But they also need to understand what the warning means, what caused it, and how it connects to the aircraft's condition. Without that understanding, the learner may perform well in a familiar drill but fail to transfer the learning to a new scenario.

A good simulation can support this by slowing down the activity, isolating key information, and asking learners to explain what they noticed. The goal is not only correct action. The goal is meaningful action.

> Practice builds fluency, but understanding builds transfer.

## Constructivism appears when learners make sense of experience

Flight simulation also connects with constructivist ideas. Learners do not simply absorb knowledge from the simulator. They interpret the experience, test assumptions, and build meaning from what happens.

A simulation becomes more useful when learners can reflect on their decisions. For example, after a scenario, an instructor might ask:

- What did you notice first?
- Why did you choose that action?
- What information did you miss?
- What would you do differently next time?

These questions help turn experience into learning. They also help learners connect theory with action.

This part of learning feels especially important because flight situations are dynamic. A learner must make judgments, not only repeat procedures. Constructivist learning gives space for interpretation, reflection, and personal meaning-making.

## Scaffolding makes simulation more humane

One of my biggest takeaways is that a simulation should not throw learners into full complexity too soon. Scaffolding matters.

A beginner may start with one instrument, one task, and one clear goal. As confidence grows, more elements can be added. The learner can move from simple scenarios to complex ones in gradual steps.

This approach respects the learner's current stage. It also reduces unnecessary mental pressure.

For example, an early simulation might focus only on maintaining altitude. Later, the learner could manage altitude while also responding to airspeed changes. After that, weather, communication, or emergency events could be added.

The simulator stays realistic, but the learning path becomes more manageable.

## My changed view of learning design

This reflection changed how I think about educational technology. I no longer see immersive tools as automatically better because they are realistic or advanced. The value depends on design.

A flight simulator can support behaviorist practice, cognitive understanding, and constructivist reflection. But it can also overload a learner if the experience is not matched to their needs.

The most useful question I am taking from T127 is simple: what should the learner notice, think about, and do at this stage?

That question moves learning design away from adding more features and toward making better choices. For me, flight simulation is a strong reminder that effective learning is not just about exposing learners to reality. It is about helping them make sense of reality, one meaningful step at a time.
