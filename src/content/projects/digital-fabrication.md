## The brief

This course explores how digital fabrication tools, from laser cutters and 3D printers to sewing and electronics, can support meaningful learning. Across the semester, I move from theory to hands-on making, design a dream toy for children, and build a final learning design that goes through prototyping, user testing, and a public expo.

## Theory into making

The course is grounded in constructionism: people learn especially well when they build things that are meaningful to them and can be shared with others. It reshaped how I start a design. Instead of asking what a learner should be told, I ask what they could make, and what that making would help them understand.

## Laser cutting

**Different Places, Same Voice.** My name tag is a window framing the three cities that shaped me, Guangzhou, London, and Boston, joined by a telephone line, because calling home has stayed constant as everything else changed. The first cut burned the wood and snapped the fragile letters, so I engraved my name instead, tested settings on small offcuts, and re-cut a base that didn't fit.

![Laser-cut wooden name tag shaped like an open window, reading 'Boie Ouyang' above the skylines of Guangzhou, London, and Boston joined by a telephone line](/images/nnSniIrsUPzNEnvmv5hWr8lAhA.webp)

**Omni-Creature.** After interviewing a classmate, I combined three parts of her identity, a favorite game character, West Lake in her hometown of Hangzhou, and her love of broccoli, into one layered creature. The hardest part was a kerf-bent wooden cylinder that wouldn't stay closed. After testing in cardboard and wood, I laser-cut rings to hold its shape, and a practical fix became part of the design.

![Layered, painted laser-cut creature combining a game character, a West Lake landscape, and a broccoli](/images/UtUX8XHkJZXyitW63auKXo8Pmsk.webp)

## 3D printing

**Construction Kit, with a partner.** This was my chance to finally build the hinge my name tag was missing. We adapted a print-in-place hinge with clips sized for 3 mm materials and designed a fixed X connector to add structure. My first print taught me tolerance in a way no screen could: a fraction of a millimeter decided whether a piece held or slipped. Once we started playing with the parts, they became a small bird we never planned to build.

![Laser-cut wooden wings, tail, and head joined by gray 3D-printed hinges and clips](/images/fab-construction-kit.webp)

## Dream toy

For children, I designed a dream toy and took it through three rounds of concepts, moving from a fantasy that could do everything to something I could actually build and test, and then to the play and learning around it.

- **Transforming Dragon Car.** A dragon car that drives, swims, and flies, with wings that fold on simple hinges, paddle wheels, a tail that swings side to side, and a smooth underside for floating.
- **Floating Dragon Car.** I narrowed the focus to really swimming: a hollow, sealed 3D-printed PETG shell for buoyancy, removable paddle wheels that work on land and in water, and optional EVA foam for stability.
- **Adventure Play Set.** I put the toy into a world, a play mat with land, water, and a mountaintop, and designed learning opportunities into it: problem solving, cause and effect, fine motor skills, and imaginative play.

![Three concept sketches of the dream toy: a transforming dragon car with drive, swim, and fly modes; a floating dragon car with paddle wheels and a sealed hull; and an adventure play set with land, water, and mountain areas](/images/fab-dream-toy-iterations.webp)

### From concept to working toy

I built the dragon car. The finished toy is a 3D-printed, hand-painted dragon with wings that lift off, a tail that doubles as the pull handle for a gearbox inside, and wheels that swap for black paddle wheels. A carved rock base gives it a piece of the play world to drive across.

![The dragon car with its paddle wheels on, sitting in a carved orange rock base, its green flame-shaped tail sticking out behind](/images/fab-dream-toy-playset.webp)

Getting there took several rounds of printing and fitting. I printed the wings and body in test filament first, then built the chassis around axles and a rubber band before moving to a geared mechanism in the final yellow body. The body alone was a six-hour print.

![Four process photos: a white 3D-printed test wing; a gray test print of the dragon body; the gray chassis upside down with axles, wheels, and a rubber band; and the yellow body from below, showing a small gearbox between the axles](/images/fab-dream-toy-process.webp)
