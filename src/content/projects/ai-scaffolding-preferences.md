## The question

Most students now use generative AI to learn, but AI "help" is usually treated as one thing. In reality, an AI tutor can help in very different ways: explaining a concept, walking through steps, or prompting you to reflect on your own thinking. I asked whether personality shapes which kind of AI support learners prefer, and how they judge the support they choose.

## My approach

I designed a scenario-based survey completed by 99 university students across eight countries and territories. Each student faced four everyday academic tasks (reading an article, interpreting a graph, solving a logic problem, and revising a paragraph) and ranked three AI support options for each: conceptual, procedural, and metacognitive.

I kept the AI responses fixed so everyone compared the same help, then collected ratings of usefulness and personal fit, a Big Five personality measure (BFI-10), and optional open-text reasons, all analyzed in SPSS.

![Survey design table: four academic tasks (read an article, interpret a graph, solve a logic problem, revise a paragraph), each with conceptual, procedural, and metacognitive AI support](/images/Jcrkbz0aVEdhszaud3KTM3qg.webp)

## Key findings

Personality mattered far less than expected. Only agreeableness showed a meaningful link: more agreeable students leaned toward reflective prompts and away from explanation-led support.

Students generally rated the help they chose highly, but usefulness consistently scored slightly above personal fit. Support can feel helpful without feeling like "my way" of learning.

Task mattered too. In the logic and math scenario, step-by-step guidance was rated clearly the most useful (6.27 out of 7, compared with 5.50 for metacognitive and 5.42 for conceptual support).

![Bar chart of mean usefulness ratings in the logic and math scenario: procedural 6.27, metacognitive 5.50, conceptual 5.42 on a 1 to 7 scale](/images/pW8IOAdAL01y1GqTASY5Dm2f574.webp)

## Design implications

Don't lock learners into support based on a personality profile. Instead, build flexible tools that let learners move between explanation, step-by-step guidance, and reflective prompting, with sensible defaults shaped by the task. Designers should also measure whether support feels right, not just whether it helps.

![Design concept 'Flexible support, not fixed personalisation': an AI tutor where the learner can switch between explaining, step-by-step guidance, and reflection](/images/o4xKKPtr0lV2ge6fV8URaCFv8.webp)

This study captured stated preferences rather than learning outcomes, so my next step is to test these ideas in an interactive AI tutor prototype.
