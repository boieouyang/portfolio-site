## The challenge

Harvard's EASEL Lab turns evidence-based social-emotional learning (SEL) programs into short, flexible activities that educators can run without a full curriculum. Our client wanted after-school staff to use them more. The research behind the activities is strong, so I focused on a different question: why do good activities still go unused, and what would make them part of an already full day?

## Who the design serves

Before talking to anyone, I mapped who the design has to work for. Students benefit, educators do the work of implementing it, and program leaders care whether it actually makes a difference. Each group defines success differently, and the people who carry the most effort, educators, have the least time.

![Three concentric rings: students at the center who benefit, after-school educators, site directors, and trainers who implement, and program leads, partners, and families who care about outcomes, with what each group values](/images/sel-stakeholders.webp)

## Listening first

I wrote the interview guides and interviewed a preschool teacher and a school leader who also runs after-school care. Instead of asking what they thought of SEL, I asked about real moments: when they reach for an activity, how they choose one, and what makes them stop.

## What I heard

- **Time is the real barrier.** When the day is full, anything that feels extra is the first thing to go.
- **SEL can feel too theoretical.** Educators want something they can run in the next five minutes, not a framework to study.
- **They choose by situation, not by skill.** A teacher starts from "two kids just had a fight," not from a list of competencies.
- **Simple wins.** Activities need few steps and a short duration, especially with young children.
- **Browsing is overwhelming.** Too many large cards at once, alphabetical order, and endless scrolling make the right activity hard to find in time.

## Reframing the problem

These conversations shifted my framing from "help teachers find SEL activities" to what I call context-responsive SEL: noticing what students need right now, judging whether an SEL activity fits, choosing one, adapting it to the time, group, and setting, and reflecting afterward.

That reframing gave me sharper design questions:

- How might we help after-school educators quickly find and adapt activities that fit their students' real-time needs?
- How might we show when and why an activity is useful, so it feels relevant rather than like extra work?
- How might we reduce the time and effort needed to select, understand, and run an activity?

## A concept direction

My proposal starts from the moment instead of the catalog. An educator says who they teach and what is happening right now, sees a few matched activities with time, steps, and materials up front, and runs one in three visual steps, with an editable, printable template for settings without a fixed classroom.

![Side-by-side concept: today, an alphabetical grid of large cards with endless scrolling; the concept, four steps from choosing an age group and a current student need to a few compact matched activities and a three-step guide with a printable template](/images/sel-concept-flow.webp)

Each of us developed an individual proposal before we synthesized them as a team.

## From concept to prototype

We turned the synthesis into **Kernels Hub**, a working prototype for afterschool educators. It opens the way my proposal did, with the moment instead of the catalog: "Tell us what's happening today," and in about a minute the educator gets a short, ready-to-use Kernel they can lead in 5 to 15 minutes. It's free and needs no sign-up.

![Kernels Hub home page: 'Make social-emotional learning part of every day', a 'Plan today' button, and four stages of support: Plan, Lead, Reflect, and Grow](/images/sel-kernels-hub.webp)

The prototype supports an educator across the whole day, not just the moment of choosing:

- **Plan, before.** Find an activity for today, personalize it with AI or rehearse it, and map out the week on a calendar.
- **Lead, during.** Step-by-step comics and short demos, screen slides and printable cards, and a quick student check-in before and after.
- **Reflect, after.** Mark each activity as used, adapted, or not used, and share what worked with other educators.
- **Grow, over time.** A 5-minute training with a certificate, a staff card game, and a wellbeing check-in for the educators themselves.

Next, we're collecting feedback through the prototype to see whether starting from a situation really makes choosing faster.

## What I'm learning

The hardest part of designing for educators isn't the content. It's the moment of use: a noisy room, a tight schedule, and a few seconds to decide. Designing for that moment, rather than for an ideal teacher with time to browse, is the shift this project taught me.
