## The brief

Every week I take one verb through the same four stages. I write to define it, then research it through seven visual elements: space, line, shape, color, value, form, and texture. From there I sketch the strongest directions and build them as interactive prototypes, using Figma for visual exploration and Figma Make to bring motion and interaction to life.

## How I take a verb apart

Every interaction answers the same questions, so I use them as a checklist for each verb:

- **Affordance and signifier:** what the interface makes possible, and what tells you so
- **Trigger:** what starts the action, the person or the system
- **Constraints and rules:** what won't work, and what the system will accept
- **Feedback:** what tells you it happened

Working through them keeps me honest about what an interface makes visible, learnable, and controllable, and what it quietly hides.

## Weekly explorations

So far I've taken six verbs through the process: peel, submit, stalk, run, confabulate, and starve. Each starts from a written definition, then becomes a set of visual studies that express it through shape, color, texture, and motion.

## Starve: designing an action that doesn't exist yet

My current verb grew out of a question about AI dependence and surveillance: what would it mean to *starve* a system that feeds on our attention? I started from ideas such as automation bias, cognitive offloading, sycophancy, the ELIZA effect, and surveillance capitalism, and from "feed" as a metaphor.

![Four word studies for STARVE: illustrative, the letters draining under a feed-level bar; abstract, a scribbled empty plate with fork and knife; emotive, the blurred word stitched shut with red thread; and antithetical, the word repeated in bright colors and covered in notification badges](/images/verbs-starve-studies.webp)

**Research.** I mapped the tactics people already use against algorithmic feeds: hiding metrics (Demetricator), confusing the profile (Go Rando, obfuscation), overfeeding it with noise (AdNauseam), limiting use (Minus), and resetting it. Every one of them still gives the system something. Starving is the gap: no click, no noise, no linger, an action that doesn't exist in today's interfaces.

![Storyboard of the feed draining: FEED 100% with '+1 bite' as a finger taps; FEED 74%, 'scroll = more food'; FEED 42% after twelve seconds of stillness; FEED 0%, 'nothing new to learn about you'](/images/verbs-starve-feed.webp)

![Storyboard of a mouth: cards pour into it with a 'gulp', then a needle stitches the lips shut, one stitch at a time, until only a sewn line remains](/images/verbs-starve-mouth.webp)

**Week 9 pin-up.** I read across Brunton and Nissenbaum on obfuscation, Zuboff on behavioral surplus, Seaver on algorithms as traps, machine unlearning, and habit research, noting where starving agrees and where it departs. Brunton and Nissenbaum's tactics, for example, still feed the machine: noise is still food. The critique pushed me in useful directions: the word is heavy, and representation was the biggest issue ("I don't want to starve the pufferfish"). The feedback asked me to try other representations, such as a data double, to treat starving as a spectrum, to look beyond social media, and to test with people.

**Where it is now.** The latest prototype takes the data-double direction: an interactive collage titled *What the machine knows about me*. Data keeps feeding a portrait of you as you move. To starve it, you catch a seal and hold, like holding your breath, and the profile stiffens and fades until *it can no longer put you together*. Next, I'm testing it with three to five people and mapping the spectrum of starving.

## What's next

Most digital learning still follows one script: read, quiz, submit. This practice has me designing for a different sequence: explore, compare, interpret, reflect, create. Learning is full of verbs, and each one deserves an interaction that invites learners to act, not just read.
